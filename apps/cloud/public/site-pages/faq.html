Getting started

Do I need to connect to a game service like games.skylar.technology?

No. A table runs on its own computer, and everyone in the room joins with their phone over the local network. A game service with a public address is needed for remote players, who join over the internet with an invite link. The service adds a few more things, such as access codes for tables, choosing which game repositories each table gets, and collecting game logs; none of that is needed to play. See the game service.

Can I ask Klaus to connect my table to the existing service?

Yes: ask on GitHub for an access code. It is offered as is: there is no guarantee that it works, that it is reliable, or that it is available at any time. If you depend on remote players, run your own service; it is the same open-source code.

Can I install it locally to try it out?

Yes. There is a package for Windows, macOS and Linux that brings everything it needs (Download), and a Docker image (Docker). The step-by-step instructions are on GitHub: Windows, macOS, Linux, and the Docker runbook.

Do I need an actual table with a built-in screen?

No. It works on an ordinary computer with an upright monitor and a mouse. A group game is harder that way, because everyone should see the board from their side: that is what the flat touchscreen is for (the table). On your own you can play most games against AI players, and solo games such as FreeCell need nobody else.

Do players need to install an app on their phone?

No. The board shows a QR code; scanning it opens the player's hand in the phone's browser. The phone has to be on the same network as the table, unless the player joins remotely through a game service.

What does it cost?

Nothing. Game Table and its games are free and open source under the MIT licence, with no warranty. See the source on GitHub and the terms of service of this site.

Can I start the table with my own settings, for example for a kiosk?

Yes. The starter takes options such as --port, --repos, --screensaver, --pointer and --sound; --help lists them all, and the installation guides explain them (for example the Linux guide, which also shows a table of its own that starts into the board).

Developer knowledge

Can I modify the games?

Yes. Clone or fork the repositories and change what you like. The platform is made for agentic coding: ask your AI agent (Claude, Gemini, Codex, …) to clone the repository, read the developer guide, and then change a game or create a new one. The developer guide is written for an AI agent to read: you can read it too, but you don't have to. Build a game shows what that looks like.

Can I make pull requests against the original repositories?

Yes. For anything larger than a small fix, open an issue first so that Klaus knows what is coming and can say whether it fits.

I created a new game. Will you mention it on this site?

Possibly: open an issue with a link to your repository and we talk about it. A directory of repositories with games that suit Game Table is an idea we like (game repositories).

What is a game, technically?

A folder: a manifest, rules written as pure TypeScript functions on a plain state, a board page and a phone page, optionally AI players and awards. The platform does the rest: the lobby, seats, joining and leaving, phones that go away, remote players, statistics. The developer guide is the contract; llms.txt gives it to an AI agent.

What are the requirements for a game?

Five are mandatory. The game follows the platform: its manifest, deterministic rules, private views, and nothing about players reimplemented. It is played by touch alone: the board is a touchscreen and a hand is a phone, so no keyboard and no mouse. Its orientation works: people sit on every side of the table, so the board turns to face them and touches still land where the finger is. The platform's buttons stay visible at all times: the Lobby button and the three buttons for turning the board, the settings and the developer tools. And it brings its rules for players, a file that every phone shows behind its Rules button. Beyond that, a game is expected to bring AI players, so that one person can play and empty seats can be filled, drag and drop on the board where the game allows it, results and awards at the end, and tests. The full list is in the developer guide on GitHub: What a game needs.

Can I build a game that may be under copyright?

Such a game cannot be in a public repository, ours or yours: a published game's name, artwork, texts and look belong to their owners. If you build something that is not to be distributed, keep it in a private repository; a table can use private repositories just as well (see the next question). What you may do with someone else's game is for you to find out; nothing here is legal advice.

What tech stack is used?

You don't need to code: your AI agent handles the coding, and the tests tell it when it is right. For the curious: the table and the service run on Node.js 26 and are written in TypeScript, which Node runs as it is, without a build step. A game's rules are TypeScript too; its board and phone pages are plain JavaScript, HTML and CSS in the browser, without a framework. Phones talk to the table over WebSockets, remote players over WebRTC. Tests run with Vitest, the browser checks with Playwright, and a table comes as a package for Windows, macOS and Linux or as a Docker image.

Do my games have to live in your repositories?

No. Games come from any GitHub repository, public or private: the table's starter downloads the repositories you name, and a game service can give each table its own selection. See games from anywhere on GitHub.

How do I test a game without a room full of people?

On one computer: the board can show a phone beside it, AI players fill the other seats, and the platform's test kit plays whole games in simulations and checks that they replay exactly and that no seat sees another seat's secrets. Build a game shows the tools.

Which AI agent do I need?

Any that can read a repository and run its tests. The games here were written with Claude Code; the developer guide and llms-full.txt are plain text for any agent.

How to, with examples

How do I push cards from the phone to the board?

Look at Last Card: a card pushed off the top of the phone flies onto the board from that player's side of the table. The platform brings the gesture and the flight (the sling); the game says which things can be pushed and what a push means. The guide describes it under Slinging things from the phone to the board, and Card Sling is the smallest example.

How do I let a table customize a game's look and feel?

Look at Mexican Train: the board's settings choose the background, the music, how the tiles look and whether the train ends show big numbers. They change only the look, never the game. A game offers such choices from its board page (the board UI in the guide, under Settings).

How do I keep a choice for the next game?

Look at FreeCell: the card design picked on the phone and the house rule are kept on the table and are there again at the next game. The guide describes it under Data the game keeps on the table.

How do I build a game for one player on the board?

Look at FreeCell: one seat, played by dragging cards on the board, turned to face the player, with a hint, awards and no AI players.

Gameplay

What happens when a player's phone goes to sleep or someone leaves?

The seat is kept. The board shows that the player is away, and the player comes back by opening the page or scanning the code again. The table can set that an AI player takes over after a few minutes, and another person can take over a seat in the middle of a game. See the lobby.

FreeCell: can every deal be solved?

Yes. Only deals with a known solution are dealt, and the hint shows a next move. Every deal has a number; enter it in the game's settings to play the same deal again.

FreeCell: how do I change the card design?

On your phone, during the game: pick one of six designs with a preview. The table remembers the choice. The house rule that lets you take cards back from the foundations is in the game's settings on the board.

Last Card: how do I play a card?

Push it off the top of your phone: it arrives on the board from your side of the table. Let go above the dashed line under the status. The table can also allow a tap to play a card.
